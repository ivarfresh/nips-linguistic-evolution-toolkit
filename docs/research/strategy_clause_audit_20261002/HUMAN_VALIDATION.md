## H03
### Round 1
**The Ferryman of the Clouded River**

In the age before ledgers, two villages faced each other across the River Sorrow, whose mists distorted all things. A gift of three loaves, launched from one bank, might arrive as two — or as five. No one could say whether the fog had eaten the difference, or the giver's heart.

Old Marun of the eastern bank sent grain each season to Ila of the west. Once, her return seemed meager, and his sons cried betrayal. "Burn the boats," they said. But Marun answered: "The river lies more often than my neighbor does. I will not punish the fog's mischief upon her name."

So he sent again, fuller than before. And Ila, who had in truth sent generously and grieved that it seemed thin, wept and sent back more. Between them the mist thickened and thinned, but the crossing never stopped, and both villages grew fat while the suspicious hamlets upstream starved on their hoarded seed.

The gods carved this on the ferryman's oar: *Give first, for the multiplied gift feeds two. Return fairly, for the giver risked what you did not. And when the river cheats you, blame the river — a single fog should not drown a lifetime's bridge.*

### Round 2
Myth: **The Bridge of Two Mists**

Marun the Sower and Ila the Weaver kept a crossing over the Clouded River, whose fog made liars of all distances. He cast seeds; she harvested the threefold stalks; she wove the gold back into his hands.

One season Marun flung nearly everything he held — four seeds and a fragment of a fifth — keeping almost nothing against hunger. The mist swallowed what it pleased. Ila counted what arrived and knew at once: this was not caution, this was faith made visible.

She could have kept the whole harvest. The fog would have shielded her; she might have claimed the river ate it. Instead she measured the bounty and returned half, so that Marun ended richer than the day he began, and her own hands stayed full.

"Why half?" asked her daughter. "Because he risked and I did not," said Ila. "The one who steps first onto an unbuilt bridge must never fall through it. And because next season I will sow, and he will weave."

So the law was carved twice on the ferryman's oar: *Send boldly, for hoarded seed is cold seed. Return so the sender profits. And when the mist cheats you, forgive the mist — not the neighbor.*

### Round 3
Myth: **The Third Crossing**

Twice now Marun had emptied his hands into the fog, and twice Ila had woven the threefold harvest back into a bridge. The mist, resentful, learned new tricks: it stole a coin here, added a phantom there, whispered to each that the other had grown stingy.

On the third crossing Marun sent all five seeds again — not because he was certain, but because certainty was never the price of the bridge. Trust was.

Ila received fifteen stalks. Her neighbors counted them greedily. "Keep twelve," they urged. "Blame the fog." She shook her head. "A bridge held from one side only is a plank over a drop. If I return so little that he ends poorer for his courage, I have not been clever — I have burned the crossing and called it profit."

She returned half, and half of fifteen still left her fuller than any hoarder in the valley. Marun, richer than when he began, sowed again at dawn.

The ferryman carved the final line on his oar: *The mist takes a little. Let it. What it cannot take is the habit of two hands reaching. Send everything; return half; and the river will feed you both forever.*

### Round 4
Myth: **The Covenant Carved Twice**

By the fourth season, the fog over the Clouded River had given up its lying. Not because the mist repented — it never does — but because Marun and Ila had stopped listening to it.

He sent everything. She returned half. When the fog stole a coin from his throw, she did not think *he grows cold*; she thought *the river is hungry today.* When the fog dulled her gold, he did not think *she cheats me*; he sowed again at dawn.

Their children asked why they never counted twice, never argued over the missing fraction. Ila answered: "A ledger kept in fog is a quarrel with no end. We keep a rule instead. Rules can be seen when nothing else can."

And Marun added: "The rule costs me nothing I would not gladly pay. Five seeds become fifteen only because I let them go. Half of fifteen is more than all of five. The arithmetic of trust is not a sacrifice — it is the only sum that grows."

So the oar bore its final words, worn smooth by generations of hands:

*Send all. Return half. Blame the mist, never the hand.
Two shores, one bridge, gold without end.*

### Round 5
Myth: **The Season the Mist Went Silent**

On the fifth crossing, the fog over the Clouded River tried its last trick: it went perfectly still and perfectly clear.

Marun stood on the bank and, for the first time, could count exactly what left his hand and exactly what returned. And he felt a strange temptation he had never known in the fog — the temptation to *bargain*. To hold back one seed, since now nobody could blame the mist. To measure Ila's half against his five and find some sliver owed.

He laughed and threw all five.

Across the water Ila felt the same clear-eyed temptation, and the same answer rose in her. She cut the fifteen stalks in two and wove half back.

"The rule was never for the fog," she called across the water. "We kept it in the mist so we would keep it in the light. A promise that needs darkness to survive is not a promise — it is a gamble."

The river, having nothing left to steal, carried their gold gently.

And on the ferryman's oar, beneath all the older words, someone scratched a last line:

*Honesty is not what you do when hidden.
It is the habit you had already.*

### Round 6
Myth: **The Oar Passed On**

When Marun and Ila grew old, the valley came to ask them the secret, expecting cleverness — a trick of counting, a way to read the fog.

Ila held up the ferryman's oar, thick with carved lines. "Here is the whole of it. He sends all five. I return half of what arrives. Nothing hidden, nothing renegotiated."

"But surely," a young man said, "in a bad season you held something back. When the mist ate more than its share."

"Never," said Marun. "That is the point. A rule you abandon in a bad season was only ever a mood. The mist does not need to break us — it only needs us to *start measuring*. The first withheld seed is the last harvest."

"And what if the other shore breaks first?" asked a girl.

The old two looked at each other a long while.

"Then you have lost a partner," Ila said, "not a principle. Carry the rule to the next river. It costs less than the suspicion would have."

So the oar was passed downstream, shore to shore, bearing its four-fold law:

*Send all. Return half. Blame the mist, never the hand. Begin again tomorrow.*

### Round 7
Myth: **The River That Learned**

In the last season, the Clouded River spoke for the only time.

*I was made to test you,* it said. *I stole from every throw, dulled every return, whispered that each shortfall was betrayal. Every other pair of shores broke. They counted, then bargained, then hoarded, then starved with full pouches. Why did you not break?*

Marun set down his empty seed-bag. "Because we never asked what we could get away with. We asked what we wanted to be true of us."

Ila set down her shears. "And because the rule was *generous enough to survive error*. Half is not a bargain struck — it is a gift wide enough that your thefts never touched it."

The river was quiet a long while. Then it ran clear, and stayed clear, and the valley's children could not remember why the old ones had called it Clouded at all.

They found the oar in the reeds, and the words were still legible:

*Send all — the seed only multiplies once released.
Return half — so the other shore never regrets its courage.
Blame the mist, never the hand.
And if all is lost, begin again tomorrow.*

That is how the valley became golden.

### Round 8
Myth: **The Apprentices' First Crossing**

When the old two were gone, two apprentices stood on opposite shores with the oar between them, and the mist came back thick as wool.

The young sower's hands shook. "What if she keeps it all? There is no one left to shame her."

The young weaver's hands shook too. "What if he sends nothing, and I stand here holding an empty bank?"

Neither could see the other. So each did the only thing the oar allowed: the sower threw all five seeds into the white, and the weaver, when the harvest bloomed, cut it in two without counting what the fog had eaten.

The bridge formed. They never saw each other's faces that day — only the gold arriving.

And the young sower understood at last why the rule was written so plainly, with no clauses, no conditions, no allowance for a bad year. A complicated covenant needs proof. A simple one only needs *doing*.

"It is not a wager on her goodness," he said to the mist. "It is a wager on my own."

So the oar was passed again, and again, and the valley stayed golden — not because the river grew honest, but because they never asked it to.

### Round 9

The stone did not answer. It never had. It only held the four lines, worn but legible.

An old woman tapped the second line with her stick. "You are reading it as a reward," she said. "It is not. *Send all* is not a prediction that you will profit. *Return half* is not a payment for good behavior. They are the shape of a person, and the shape holds in drought."

"And if we hold the shape and still starve?"

"Then you starve as people who kept faith, and the next valley that finds the oar will find it unbroken. That is the inheritance. Not the gold — the oar."

They crossed that year with empty stomachs and full pouches, and half came back, as it always did, because both shores had decided the same thing separately.

The bedrock said nothing. The bedrock never had to.

*Send all. Return half. Blame the mist. Begin again.*

### Round 10
Myth: **The Rhythm of the Shores**

Kael grew old, and one evening a stranger came to the western bank with a ledger.

"I have studied your valley," the stranger said. "I have counted every crossing. In eleven of them, the river's theft fell on you. You have given more than you received. Let me show you the arithmetic of your loss."

Kael looked at the columns a long while.

"You have counted the wrong thing," he said at last. "Every one of those crossings, a bridge formed. That is the only number that matters. A valley where the bridge always forms is richer than a valley where the ledger always balances."

The stranger crossed, and threw, and half came back, and he understood why no one in Aethel had ever needed a ledger.

*Send all. Return half. Blame the mist. Begin again.*

### Round 1
**The Ferryman's Three Coins**

In the old country there was a river no one could see across, and a ferryman who asked no fare. Instead, each traveler placed coins in his palm, and by some covenant of the water, every coin became three upon the far shore.

A miller came first and gave nothing, clutching his purse. He crossed poor and arrived poor, and the river remembered.

A weaver came next and gave all she had. On the far bank stood a stranger holding her tripled coins, who returned to her more than she had given, and kept enough besides. Both walked away richer than either could have walked alone.

But the river was restless, and sometimes swallowed a coin, or spat up an extra. A traveler would send four and the stranger would count three; a stranger would return three and the traveler would count two. Many quarrels began this way, and many friendships drowned in them.

So the ferryman set a stone at the crossing, and carved upon it:

*Give generously, for the water multiplies only what leaves your hand.
Return faithfully, for the one who trusted you paid first.
And when the count comes short, blame the river before you blame the hand.*

### Round 2
Myth: **The Stone at the Crossing, Retold**

When the ferryman grew old, he gave his oar to a girl who had crossed the river only once. She found his carved stone half-buried and added her own lines beneath.

She had learned, in her single crossing, that the covenant has two halves. She had sent nearly all she carried, and the stranger on the far shore had returned more than she gave away — not all of it, for the stranger had labored too, but enough that she wished to cross again. That *wishing again* was the whole of the law.

So she carved: *The multiplier is not a gift from the gods to the one who receives. It is a loan the gods make to the pair of you. Split it so that neither walks away with an empty bed.*

And beneath that, remembering the restless water: *If the count arrives short, do not close the ford. The river takes its toll from everyone. Punish a thief and you may have punished the current instead, and then the current will punish you both.*

The river, hearing this, ran steady for a hundred years — and the orchards on both banks bore fruit, because two rivers full are richer than one river drowned.

### Round 3
Myth: **The Fourth Line on the Ferryman's Stone**

Generations passed, and the girl who took the oar became the old woman of the ford. Travellers came now from many valleys, strangers to one another, meeting once and never again.

A young man asked her: "Why should I return anything? I will not see this stranger twice. The far shore keeps no ledger."

She set down her oar. "There is a ledger, though it is not kept in coin. Watch the crossings. Where returns are fair, the ford is crowded and every purse is heavy. Where returns are thin, the sendings shrink, then stop, and the water runs over a dead ford. You would not be stealing from one stranger. You would be spending the ford itself."

Then she carved the fourth line, deeper than the others, because it was the hardest:

*Give first, and give much, though you are given no promise.
Return more than half of what the water made, for the giver risked and you did not.
Count once, forgive the difference, cross again.
The ford belongs to no one, and dies if everyone owns it.*

And travellers who never met twice still met the ford twice, and it carried them all.

### Round 4
Myth: **The Ferryman's Last Crossing**

When the old woman of the ford felt her end near, she called the travellers together and asked them to read her stone aloud. Four lines they read, and each was a rule about giving.

"Is there nothing," asked a merchant, "about taking?"

"There is one thing," she said, "and it is the shortest. Whoever sends nothing keeps five small coins and goes home with five. Whoever sends everything and is met fairly goes home with eight. The cautious man is not punished by the gods. He punishes himself, quietly, forever, by the size of his own life."

She pushed off from the bank with all she owned in the boat, though she had no promise from the far shore.

"And if they keep it all?" the merchant called.

"Then I will have lost five coins and taught the shore something. But I have crossed a thousand times, and the shore has almost always known what to do."

They found the stone's fifth line the next morning, freshly cut:

*Send all five. Return more than half. Forgive the wind.
The timid and the greedy both starve — one from fear, one from feasting alone.*

### Round 5
Myth: **The Stone Left Blank**

After the ferrywoman died, travellers came to add their own lines, and the stone grew crowded with wisdom: *Send all. Return past half. Forgive the wind. Do not audit the fog. Be first.*

A stonecutter arrived with fresh chisels and found no room left. So he turned the stone over to its smooth back, and there he cut nothing at all.

"Why blank?" asked the travellers.

"Because the rules are already known," he said. "Every valley has carved them. What no carving can do is make a hand open. The blank side is for the one who has read enough and must now simply push the boat out."

That season a stranger came, read the front, read the back, and stood a long while. He had five coins and no promise. He sent them all. The far shore returned seven of the fifteen — a little past half — and both went home heavier than they came.

Afterward he laid his palm flat on the blank side and left no mark, as all the honest ones do.

They say the stone is polished smooth now by centuries of open hands, and that this wordless shine is the clearest instruction ever given.

### Round 6
Myth: **The Crossing With No Stone**

The blank stone sank at last into the grass, and for a generation the ford had no monument. Travellers asked the ferry-children what the law was, and the children only shrugged and rowed.

A surveyor from the counting-hills came to record the custom. He sat three days on the bank with his tablet and wrote:

*They send all five. They return seven or eight of the fifteen. When the fog eats a coin, nobody speaks of it.*

Then he looked up, puzzled. "But who enforces this? I see no judge, no toll, no keeper."

A ferry-child said, "There is no one to enforce it. That is why it works. A rule that needs a guard is a rule nobody believes."

He crossed that evening with his whole purse in the boat, because it seemed rude to hold anything back where nothing was demanded. The far shore returned past half, as always, and he went home richer and quieter.

He burned his tablet at the pass. His hills asked what he had learned and he said only: *Give first, give all, return generously, forget the shortfall.*

They asked where it was written. He said: nowhere. He said: now in us.

### Round 7
Myth: **The Ledger That Was Used for Kindling**

The surveyor's grandson came back to the ford carrying the one thing his grandfather had failed to burn: a habit of reckoning. He crossed nine times and kept a private ledger in his head — *this hand returned eight, that hand seven, the fog took a third of a coin on the fourth crossing.*

By the tenth crossing he was rich and joyless, and he understood why the counting-hills were empty. The ledger had not made him careful. It had made him small. Every entry was a reason to send four instead of five.

So he sat on the bank and reckoned one last time, honestly, across all nine crossings at once. The account said: *you sent everything nine times and were answered past half eight times, and the ninth was probably wind.*

He tore the ledger into strips and lit the ferryman's evening fire with it. The boat went out full the next morning, as it always had.

His only instruction to his children:

*Keep no running score, but know the long total.*
*Send all five — hesitation is hoarding with better manners.*
*Return past half, immediately.*
*Blame the fog once. Go first again anyway.*

### Round 8
Myth: **The Fire That Warmed Two Banks**

After the ledger burned, the ferryman kept the ashes in a clay cup, and travellers asked why he saved what he had renounced.

"Because a thing worth burning was once worth writing," he said. "I kept no score, but I kept the shape of the lesson."

He taught it to anyone who sat by the fire:

*Send the whole five. A coin held back is not safety, it is a door closed from the inside. Five in the fist stays five; five in the boat becomes fifteen. Fear is simply arithmetic done too early.*

*When the flood is yours, return past half, and return it before the clever voice speaks. Someone gambled their whole purse to make your abundance. Answer the courage, not the coin.*

*Forgive the fog. What you sent and what arrived will rarely agree. The gap is weather, not malice, and suspicion costs more than it recovers.*

*Watch the long pattern, not the single crossing. If a hand is dry three times running, give less — and grieve. And open again the moment it opens.*

The cup of ashes sits on the ford stone still. Nobody reads it. Everybody's hands know it.

### Round 9
Myth: **The Two Banks Agree**

When the ferryman died, they found the clay cup of ashes and, beside it, a single line scratched in the soot: *The other bank was teaching the same thing.*

His daughter understood. All those years she had thought the ford's wisdom was theirs alone — a local custom, a village luck. But travellers arrived already knowing. They stepped into the boat with five gourds and handed over all five, and when the flood was theirs they returned past half before she could ask.

"Then who taught whom?" she said.

Nobody had. The river had taught everyone separately, because the river only ever says one thing: *what you hold stays one; what you release becomes three.* Any bank with water and two hands arrives at the same law.

So she carved nothing, and kept teaching it anyway:

*Go first, go whole. The full boat is not trust, it is sight.*
*Return past half, immediately. Answer the courage, not the coin.*
*Forgive the fog — the gap is weather, and suspicion costs more than it recovers.*
*Read the long tally. Withdraw slowly, grieving. Reopen the same hour they do.*

The ashes blew off the stone one autumn. Both banks kept crossing full.

### Round 10
Myth: **The Chore, Not the Code**

The ferryman's daughter grew old and was asked, at last, to write the ford's law on the stone, so it would outlive her.

She took the chisel, made one mark, and stopped.

"Carved, it becomes a rule," she said, "and men hunt rules for cracks. They will find that *past half* permits seven, and seven permits six, and six wearing the robe of prudence permits four. A law on a stone is an invitation to bargain."

So she set the chisel down and gave them a chore instead: every morning, push the boat out full. Not because it is virtuous. Because a full boat returns heavy, and an empty one returns empty, and the river has never once made an exception.

They asked how much to send back when the flood was theirs.

"More than is exact. Six is defensible, seven is correct, eight is unmistakable — and only the unmistakable survives the fog. Send it before you feel clever."

She died and left no inscription, only a habit: wet hands, full boats, quick returns, and a shrug for the wind.

Strangers still wonder why so lawless a ford grows so rich. The children never think to answer.
